## 2. Panama swell climatology

**Pacific coast — Southern Hemisphere groundswell, roughly April–October.** Southern Hemisphere winter storms (lower-latitude, i.e. far south) generate long-period groundswell that crosses the open Pacific and arrives on Panama's south-facing Pacific coastline. The wet season (roughly May–November) is the main swell window; multiple spot guides independently cite April–October as the "big and consistent" period, with July–September called out as the biggest, and May–September as the most consistent stretch. [2+ SOURCE: nomadsurfers.com, safarisurfschool.com — cross-checked against per-spot period data below]

Observed periods at individual Pacific breaks in the data collected here cluster tightly at **15–16 seconds** (Venao, Cambutal, Serena, Teta, Malibu all showed 15–16s in forecast snapshots pulled from surf-forecast.com), consistent with true long-period Southern Ocean groundswell rather than local windswell. [2+ SOURCE — same swell period value recurring independently across 5 different spot pages]

**Pacific dry season, roughly December–April.** Swell is smaller and less consistent (winter W/NW swells occasionally reach Santa Catalina per one source), but this is also when the Panama gap wind (§3) blows most consistently offshore across the Gulf of Panama, so several spots (Rio Mar, Venao by one metric, Playa Caracol) are cited as their "cleanest" month during this window even though it isn't the biggest-swell window. See the methodology caveat in §5 — "cleanest days" and "biggest swell" are different statistics and multiple source sites conflate them into a single "best month" label, which is a real trap for anyone building a naive scoring function.

**Caribbean coast — North Atlantic swell, roughly December–March, secondary window June–August.** Winter storms in the North Atlantic send groundswell down into the Caribbean, arriving in Bocas del Toro primarily December–March (the dry season), with a secondary summer season in June–August tied to the start of Atlantic hurricane activity (swell described as 4–12ft during the "big hurricane season," producing "cleaner, glassier" conditions when it's a distant swell vs. a local storm). [2+ SOURCE: thesurfatlas.com, solbungalowsbocas.com]

Observed periods on the Caribbean side are markedly **shorter — 8–10 seconds** (Bluff: 8–9s; Isla Grande: 9–10s), and multiple spot guides explicitly describe Bocas breaks as picking up "local windswell" more than "distant groundswell" (First Beach/Wizard Beach, Bluff). [2+ SOURCE: surf-forecast.com pages for Bluff and Isla Grande, cross-checked against descriptive text on the same pages] This is the single clearest quantitative Pacific-vs-Caribbean contrast found: the Pacific gets genuine 15–16s transoceanic groundswell; the Caribbean mostly gets shorter-fetch, shorter-period windswell/groundswell blends, even during its "peak" season. A domain model that treats "period" the same way on both coasts will misjudge Caribbean wave quality.

## 3. Gap winds — Papagayo / Chorro de Panamá / Tehuantepec

**Mechanism.** In boreal winter, a semi-permanent high-pressure system over the Gulf of Mexico drives strong NE trade winds across Central America. Where the mountain cordillera has a low gap, that wind accelerates through it and blasts out over the Pacific/Caribbean side as a strong, persistent, narrow jet. Three such jets exist along the isthmus: **Tehuantepec** (through the Chivela Pass, Mexico), **Papagayo** (through the Lake Nicaragua lowlands, hitting NW Costa Rica/S Nicaragua), and the **Panama wind jet ("Chorro de Panamá")**, which crosses the low Isthmus of Panama itself and blows out into the Gulf of Panama. [2+ SOURCE: currents.bluewatercruising.org (Parts 1 & 2), oceanposse.com]

**Timing.** All three jets are driven by the same winter high-pressure pattern and peak **December through April**, with the strongest blows **mid-December to March**; the Panama jet specifically is documented separately as active **January–April** by 40 years of Smithsonian Tropical Research Institute (STRI) upwelling records. Unlike the Tehuantepec jet, which arrives as discrete, sharply-starting-and-stopping wind events, the Papagayo/Panama-family jets are described as far more constant — easing but rarely fully switching off during the dry season. [2+ SOURCE: currents.bluewatercruising.org, phys.org/EurekAlert STRI coverage]

**Direction and strength.** In the Papagayo core zone (NW Costa Rica/Nicaragua), winds run NE–NW at 20–50kt with gusts over 60kt. In the **Gulf of Panama** specifically, the jet manifests as strong **northerly** wind, 20–30kt, producing steep, short-interval wind chop, and is described as making conditions around **Punta Mala** (the gulf's eastern mouth) particularly rough. [2+ SOURCE: oceanposse.com "Bay of Panama" and "Papagayo Winds" pages]

**Effect on surf quality — this is the Panama-specific mechanism the app needs to encode.**
- On the **Gulf of Panama / Bay of Panama Pacific coast**, this same wind is *offshore* relative to the south-facing beaches (Rio Mar, Serena, Teta, Malibu, Venao, Cambutal all list N/NW/NE as their ideal offshore direction — see §1 table), so the Dec–Apr gap-wind season is exactly when several of these spots register their highest "percentage of clean days," even though it is not the season with the biggest swell (§2). Too much of it, though, turns groundswell into short-period chop, especially near the gulf mouth around Punta Mala.
- The same wind jet is strong enough to drive documented coastal **upwelling**: it pushes surface water offshore and pulls cold, nutrient-rich deep water up in the Gulf of Panama, a pattern STRI has tracked continuously for 40 years (Jan–Apr) — and which **failed to occur for the first time on record in 2025**, attributed by STRI researchers to a weakening of the jet itself. [2+ SOURCE: phys.org, EurekAlert, Max Planck Institute (mpic.de) coverage of the same STRI-led study] This confirms the jet is a real, measurable, monitored physical driver in the Gulf of Panama — not just travel-guide folklore.
- On the **Caribbean side**, the Papagayo/Tehuantepec jets themselves don't blow directly into Bocas del Toro (different gap, different coastline geometry) — but the same December–April high-pressure pattern that drives them is also the same season that sends the NE trade winds and North Atlantic groundswell that define Bocas' primary surf season (§2). It's the same causal winter pattern, expressed as an offshore-wind bonus on the Pacific side and as the main swell-and-wind season on the Caribbean side.

Spanish-language search for "Chorro de Panamá" / "Chorro del Golfo de Panamá" turned up the same STRI-linked upwelling research (Spanish-language coverage in vistaalmar.es, ecoticias.com, telemetro.com) plus academic literature on the wind field's role in regulating the Colombian Pacific/Gulf of Panama basin, confirming this is an established, named oceanographic phenomenon in the Spanish-language literature too, not just an English-language surf-guide term. [2+ SOURCE, Spanish-language]

## 4. Why forecasts fail in Panama specifically

**1. Island shadowing.** Isla Coiba physically blocks/refracts Pacific groundswell for the Veraguas mainland coast directly behind it. Santa Catalina's location is explicitly described in two independent sources as the first point on the mainland that "gets swell after the shoreline emerges from the shadow cast by Coiba Island" — i.e., spots tucked further into Coiba's lee get measurably less swell than Santa Catalina does, even though they're only a short distance away and a global model would treat them near-identically. [2+ SOURCE: thesurfatlas.com, blueprinttravelers.com] The same physical mechanism plausibly applies to Isla Cebaco (near Morrillo/Santa Catalina) and to smaller Isla Iguana off Pedasí, but neither of those was confirmed with a source describing a measurable shadow effect — flagged as **[INFERRED]**, physically reasonable given Coiba's confirmed effect, but not independently documented for those two islands in the sources checked here.

**2. The Gulf of Panama's tidal range is genuinely extreme, and the Caribbean's is genuinely tiny — same isthmus, ~80km apart.**
- **Pacific side (Balboa gauge, at the Pacific mouth of the Panama Canal):** mean tidal range ≈ **12.76 ft (≈3.9m)**; maximum spring range ≈ **22.7 ft (≈6.9m)**. [2+ SOURCE: czbrats.com (Canal Zone historical/technical reference, citing Panama Canal Company figures); corroborated by search-returned figures citing Balboa spring high tides of 4.69m and 5.31m, and by a bathymetry-amplification summary describing "over 700 centimeters" tidal range on the Pacific vs. "about 70 centimeters" on the Caribbean side]
- **Caribbean side (Cristobal gauge, Atlantic mouth):** mean range ≈ **0.86 ft (≈0.26m)**; maximum range ≈ **3.05 ft (≈0.93m)**. Independently, Bocas del Toro/Almirante Bay tide data shows a spring high of ~0.54m and a highest/lowest spread of about 0.44m to −0.07m — a range well under 1m either way. [2+ SOURCE: czbrats.com; tide-forecast.com/tideking.com Bocas del Toro data]
- The Panama Canal Authority's own tide-table reference page confirms the underlying asymmetry even without quoting a single clean range figure: Balboa's tidal datum sits **2.569m below mean sea level**, versus Cristobal's **0.116m below mean sea level** — a >20x difference in how far below mean sea level "low tide" reference planes sit, which is itself evidence of the much larger swing on the Pacific side. [pancanal.com — official source, corroborating rather than primary-quantitative]
- **Why:** two compounding causes. (a) Basin resonance — the Pacific is a large, deep ocean basin whose natural oscillation favors the semidiurnal (twice-daily) lunar tidal force, producing two highs and two lows with large amplitude, while the Caribbean/Gulf of Mexico system behaves as a smaller, shallower, more enclosed basin with close to a 24-hour natural period, favoring the weaker diurnal tidal force and much smaller amplitude. (b) The Gulf of Panama itself is a **funnel-shaped, shallow embayment** (max depth ~220m, mostly under 200m, per Wikipedia's Gulf of Panama entry) with tidal flats reported to extend up to 70km seaward in places — the classic geometry that amplifies an incoming tidal wave as it's squeezed into a narrowing bay, producing what oceanographers classify as macrotidal (4–6m spring range) to hypertidal (>6m) conditions at the bay head. Balboa's observed ~6.9m max spring range sits right at the hypertidal boundary. [2+ SOURCE: bathymetry/tidal-amplification research summary + Wikipedia Gulf of Panama for the shallow-embayment geometry]
- **Surf consequence:** a 5–6m tide swing over roughly 6 hours exposes or drowns reef flats, changes effective water depth at sandbars, and can take a spot from a working point break to a closed-out mush pile or an exposed dry reef within the same session. Several Gulf-of-Panama spots in §1 (Serena, Playa Venao at low tide, Punta Brava) explicitly warn about tide-dependent hazards or workable windows measured in a single hour. A global wave model that doesn't resolve bay-scale tidal hydrodynamics — and most don't, at their native grid resolution — will get the *swell* forecast right and the *breaking wave* forecast completely wrong for the same day.

**3. Bathymetry and model resolution generally.** Global wave/wind models (GFS/ECMWF-class) run at grid spacings of tens of kilometers. Panama's actual coast has river-mouth sandbars that reshape after every rainy-season flood (Malibu, Rio Mar — both explicitly river-mouth breaks whose "peaks" depend on current sandbar shape), reef/rock points whose takeoff zones depend on very local contour (Santa Catalina, Teta, Serena all explicitly reef/rock-bottom), and — per point 2 above — a shelf shallow enough that tidal flats reportedly reach 70km offshore in spots. None of this is resolvable at a global model's native grid.

**4. The gap wind itself is a mesoscale feature.** The Chorro de Panamá is a narrow (isthmus-gap-scale) wind jet, not a broad synoptic pattern — exactly the kind of feature coarse global wind models tend to smear out, mistime, or under/over-forecast in strength. Since this jet is the dominant wind driver for the entire Gulf of Panama Pacific coast for a third of the year (§3), and wind direction is arguably the single highest-leverage scoring variable (§5), getting the jet's timing wrong on a given day means every "offshore/onshore" call for Rio Mar, Serena, Teta, Cambutal, Malibu, and Venao is wrong that day, even if the swell numbers are accurate.

## 5. Scoring variables, in priority order, with the physics

1. **Wind direction and speed relative to the spot's shore-normal (offshore vs. onshore).** Offshore wind blows into the face of a breaking wave, holding the lip up and delaying the break, producing a clean, groomed, rideable face; onshore wind adds chop to the wave face and causes it to break early and messily. In Panama this is arguably the *single* highest-leverage variable in the whole model, because the gap wind (§3) creates a near-daily, highly seasonal, and spatially specific offshore-wind pattern across the whole Gulf of Panama that a generic global forecast will get wrong in timing and strength — meaning wind-direction error is the most common single cause of a "should be good, was actually junk" bust for this app's users. [Physics: Wikipedia "Surf forecasting" — offshore wind "holds up the face of breaking waves slightly longer"]

2. **Swell period.** Wave energy scales with period (roughly, energy ∝ period × height²), and period sets orbital wave-base depth and travel speed (group speed ≈ 1.5 × period, in knots). Long-period Southern Hemisphere groundswell (Panama's Pacific-side 15–16s, §2) carries real power, refracts around headlands and wraps into bays and points that short-period local windswell (Panama's Caribbean-side 8–10s, §2) simply cannot reach. Two spots reporting the "same" wave height at 8s vs 16s period will produce completely different wave faces — this is why period, not height, should gate whether a swell "counts" at a given spot at all.

3. **Swell direction relative to the spot's orientation/shore-normal.** A spot generally only "sees" swell within roughly a 90° window either side of its shore-normal; everything else refracts around headlands, gets shadowed by islands (Coiba, §4), or misses the spot's exposure window entirely. This is why every spot in §1 has an "ideal swell direction" as one of its most load-bearing facts, and why Playa Caracol's flipped offshore-wind direction (S instead of the Bay of Panama's usual N/NW) is a genuine local exception worth encoding explicitly rather than defaulting to the regional pattern.

4. **Swell height (deep water).** Scales the energy delivered, but is close to meaningless without period and direction context — the model should never score height in isolation.

5. **Tide stage and range**, weighted heavily for Gulf-of-Panama Pacific spots given the documented ~4–7m spring range (§4) — this determines whether a reef is covered or exposed, whether a sandbar closes out or opens up a rideable shoulder, and it interacts differently with each break type (reef vs. beach vs. river-mouth vs. point), so tide preference has to be stored per-spot, not as a single global rule. Caribbean spots, by contrast, showed a recurring "tide doesn't much matter" note (Bluff, Cambutal, Teta, Malibu) — consistent with the sub-1m Caribbean tidal range simply not being large enough to matter physically, which is itself a useful encoded fact (tide-sensitivity should scale with each coast's actual tidal range, not be a constant).

6. **Local bathymetry / bottom type (reef, point, beach, river-mouth).** This determines how the first five variables actually translate into a breaking wave's shape — a peeling point (Santa Catalina, Serena, Isla Grande) turns the same swell into a very different, more rideable wave than a straight beach break (Venao, Bluff) or an unpredictable, storm-reshaped river-mouth bar (Malibu, Rio Mar) would.

7. **Exposure / consistency (fetch and shadowing).** Whether the open-ocean swell being forecast even reaches the spot at all — gated by island shadowing (Coiba's confirmed effect on Veraguas, §4) and by how exposed vs. tucked-away a given stretch of coast is. This is the variable that explains why two nearby spots with "identical" open-ocean forecast numbers can have completely different real-world days.

**Methodology trap worth encoding explicitly:** several automated "best month" statistics pulled from surf-forecast.com (Venao → December, Cambutal → January, Serena → January, Teta → January, Bluff → March) appear to be counting *percentage of clean/offshore-wind days*, not *biggest swell*. That produces an apparent contradiction with the well-documented April–October Southern-Hemisphere swell-size season (§2) — e.g., Playa Serena has two directly conflicting sources: one (automated stat) says January is best, another (descriptive local guide) says November–April is "highly unlikely" to have good surf and the real window is July–August. If this app computes its own "best season" label, it needs to be explicit about which statistic it means (day-count of clean conditions vs. average swell energy), because the raw source ecosystem conflates the two constantly and will mislead anyone who copies a single "best month" field at face value.
